# CS425: Week 11 — Stream processing, Pregel graph processing, network structure, and cluster scheduling

Week 11 (Nov 2–8) is the course's turn to "the new age" of distributed systems. The syllabus row groups stream processing, distributed graph processing, the structure of networks, scheduling, and a Storm demo, with Storm and the Pregel paper as readings and the Spark Streaming report as optional ([CS425 syllabus, Course Schedule, Week 11](https://app.notion.com/p/3c903d35d6838015b190fd706d1c7b74)). On the FA2026 lectures page these lectures (L23 and L26) are still marked Tentative with no PDFs, so this note works from the FA2025 final decks for the same lectures (L22 Structure of Networks, L22.B Stream Processing, L23 Scheduling, L26 Graph Processing, and the L26.B Spark video deck) plus the primary papers and Storm docs. The four topics look unrelated, but they share one idea: once a job runs on hundreds of machines, the structure you choose for computation (a topology of bolts, a sequence of supersteps, a queue of tenants) decides how it fails, how it recovers, and who waits.

## Stream processing: why MapReduce isn't enough, and how Storm is built

MapReduce is batch processing: you wait for the entire computation on a large dataset to finish, and it isn't meant for long-running jobs that must answer within a few seconds at high throughput (trending topics, website analytics, intrusion detection) ([L22.B](https://courses.grainger.illinois.edu/cs425/fa2025/L22.B.FA25.pdf)). Storm's vocabulary is small and examinable. A **tuple** is an ordered list of elements, a **stream** is a potentially unbounded sequence of tuples, a **spout** is a source of streams, a **bolt** consumes streams and emits new ones (filter, join, transform), and a **topology** is the directed graph of spouts and bolts that makes up one application. A topology may contain cycles if the application needs them ([L22.B](https://courses.grainger.illinois.edu/cs425/fa2025/L22.B.FA25.pdf); [Storm Concepts](https://storm.apache.org/releases/current/Concepts.html)).

Parallelism comes from splitting a bolt into many tasks, and the **grouping** decides which task gets each tuple. Shuffle grouping spreads tuples evenly (round-robin in the lecture's framing). Fields grouping partitions by a subset of fields, so every tuple with the same key reaches the same task. All grouping sends every tuple to every task. The gotcha is that any stateful bolt, such as a word counter, must use fields grouping on its key. Under shuffle grouping, counts for "the" scatter across tasks and no single task holds the right answer. The cluster side mirrors patterns you've seen all semester. **Nimbus** is the leader that distributes code, assigns tasks, and monitors failures. A **Supervisor** on each worker machine runs executors that hold groups of tasks. **ZooKeeper** coordinates Nimbus and Supervisors and holds their state, which is why either daemon can restart without losing the cluster's picture of itself ([L22.B](https://courses.grainger.illinois.edu/cs425/fa2025/L22.B.FA25.pdf)).

### Reliability: anchoring, acking, and the XOR trick

The most testable mechanism of the week is Storm's at-least-once guarantee. Every spout tuple roots a tree of descendant tuples. A tuple counts as failed when that tree isn't fully processed within a timeout, which defaults to 30 seconds through `TOPOLOGY_MESSAGE_TIMEOUT_SECS`. On failure the spout replays the root ([Storm, Guaranteeing Message Processing](https://storm.apache.org/releases/current/Guaranteeing-message-processing.html)). Bolts take part through the `OutputCollector` API. `emit` anchors an output to one or more inputs. `ack` says this bolt has finished with a tuple. `fail` fails the root at once, so the replay starts sooner than it would after a timeout. The slides warn that each tuple holds memory until it is acked or failed, so forgetting either one leaks memory ([L22.B](https://courses.grainger.illinois.edu/cs425/fa2025/L22.B.FA25.pdf)).

The efficiency trick is worth being able to explain. Every tuple gets a random 64-bit id. Special **acker** tasks, chosen by mod-hashing the spout tuple id, track each tree using a fixed amount of state, about 20 bytes per spout tuple. The main piece is an "ack val," which is the XOR of every tuple id that has been created or acked in the tree. Each id goes into the XOR twice, once when it is created and once when it is acked, so the ack val returns to zero exactly when the whole tree is done, however large the tree is ([Storm docs](https://storm.apache.org/releases/current/Guaranteeing-message-processing.html)). There are two gotchas. Emitting without an anchor means a downstream failure won't replay the root. A tuple anchored to several inputs that then fails replays several spout tuples. And because the guarantee is at-least-once, your bolts must tolerate duplicates. The docs point to Trident when you need exactly-once. Optional context from the slides: Twitter's Heron replaced Storm's acking inefficiencies with **backpressure**, where a congested stage tells upstream stages to slow down ([L22.B](https://courses.grainger.illinois.edu/cs425/fa2025/L22.B.FA25.pdf)).

### The other design: Spark Streaming's discretized streams

The optional reading explains the alternative to processing one record at a time. Zaharia et al. argue that record-at-a-time systems recover through replication or upstream backup. Replication doubles the hardware, and upstream backup recovers slowly, and neither handles stragglers well. Discretized streams (D-Streams) instead chop the stream into short batches and treat each as a deterministic batch computation over RDDs. The target latency is 0.5–2 seconds, and the authors report sub-second latency on 100 nodes ([Zaharia et al., EECS-2012-259](https://www2.eecs.berkeley.edu/Pubs/TechRpts/2012/EECS-2012-259.pdf)). Because each RDD remembers its **lineage**, the chain of coarse-grained transformations that built it, a lost partition is recomputed in parallel across many nodes instead of being replayed by one standby node. Slow tasks get speculative copies, just as in MapReduce. The required Spark video deck makes the same lineage point for batch Spark: log the transformation, recompute only what's lost, and pay nothing when nothing fails ([L26.B Spark](https://courses.grainger.illinois.edu/cs425/fa2025/L26.B.FA25.pdf)). A good exam contrast is that Storm gives per-tuple latency with at-least-once replay, while D-Streams give batch-interval latency with deterministic recomputation, and that recomputation is what makes exactly-once output natural for them.

## Graph processing: think like a vertex

Large graphs such as the web, social networks, and road networks won't fit on one server, and running MapReduce iteratively fits them badly. Each iteration becomes a Hadoop stage that ships every vertex over the network and writes everything to HDFS ([L26](https://courses.grainger.illinois.edu/cs425/fa2025/L26.FA25.pdf)). The lecture frames each iteration as **gather–apply–scatter**. A vertex gathers its neighbors' values, applies a computation to them and its own value, and scatters its new value to its neighbors.

Pregel turns this into Valiant's **Bulk Synchronous Parallel** model. Computation runs in **supersteps**. In superstep *S*, the user's `Compute()` runs at each active vertex. It reads the messages sent to that vertex in superstep *S − 1*, updates its value, and sends messages that arrive in superstep *S + 1*. A barrier separates supersteps ([Malewicz et al., SIGMOD 2010](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1807167.1807184)). Every vertex starts active. A vertex deactivates itself with `VoteToHalt()` and becomes active again only if a message arrives. The job ends when every vertex is inactive and no messages are in transit, and both conditions matter. Within a superstep, messages to a vertex arrive in no guaranteed order, but they aren't duplicated. By default the vertex-to-partition mapping is `hash(ID) mod N`, and users can override it for locality ([Pregel](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1807167.1807184)). The lecture warns that clever locality partitioning can cost more up front than it saves ([L26](https://courses.grainger.illinois.edu/cs425/fa2025/L26.FA25.pdf)).

Three features from the paper are useful for programming and for exams. **Combiners** merge messages headed to the same vertex, for example taking only the minimum for shortest paths. They are off by default and are only safe when the operation is commutative and associative, because Pregel promises nothing about which messages get grouped or in what order. **Aggregators** give global communication. Each vertex contributes a value in superstep *S*, and the reduced result (min, max, sum) is visible to everyone in *S + 1*. That makes them the natural way to detect global convergence. **Topology mutations** let vertices add or remove edges and vertices, with conflicts resolved in a fixed order in which removals come before additions ([Pregel §3](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1807167.1807184)).

For fault tolerance, the leader (the paper says "master") tells workers to checkpoint their partitions at the start of some supersteps, saving vertex values, edge values, and incoming messages. It detects failed workers with pings. When a worker fails, the leader reassigns its partitions and *every* worker rolls back to the latest checkpoint. That checkpoint may be several supersteps old, and the paper picks checkpoint frequency from a mean-time-to-failure model. The paper's "confined recovery" would roll back only the lost partitions, but it requires a deterministic algorithm and was still being developed ([Pregel §4.2](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/1807167.1807184)). Numbers from the lecture worth remembering: single-source shortest paths on a 1-billion-vertex tree took 180 s on 50 workers and 20 s on 800, and a 50-billion-vertex graph took about 700 s on 800 workers ([L26](https://courses.grainger.illinois.edu/cs425/fa2025/L26.FA25.pdf)).

## Structure of networks: small world versus power law

This lecture is light on mechanism but heavy on definitions that get tested. Two properties describe a network. The **clustering coefficient** is the probability that A and B are connected given that both connect to C. **Path length** is the length of shortest paths. A ring lattice has high clustering and long paths. A random graph has low clustering and short paths. A **small-world** network has both high clustering and short paths, and you get one by rewiring a few lattice edges to random endpoints ([L22](https://courses.grainger.illinois.edu/cs425/fa2025/L22.FA25.pdf)). **Power-law** (scale-free) networks have a degree distribution where the probability of degree *k* falls off like *k* to the minus alpha. That is a heavy tail, so on a log-log plot it's a straight line instead of an exponential cliff. Preferential attachment, where a new vertex links to an existing vertex with probability proportional to that vertex's degree, produces this. The slides give the WWW's alpha as about 2.1–2.4 and note that Gnutella has about 3.4 edges per vertex regardless of scale.

The traps are that the two properties are independent. Co-author networks are small-world without being power-law, and a disconnected power-law graph isn't small-world. The systems lesson is about resilience. Removing many random nodes rarely disconnects such a graph, but removing a few high-degree hubs does. Shortest-path routing funnels traffic through those same hubs, so the lecture suggests adding randomness to path selection ([L22](https://courses.grainger.illinois.edu/cs425/fa2025/L22.FA25.pdf)).

## Scheduling: from FIFO to dominant resource fairness

The single-processor material is warm-up, but the arithmetic shows up on quizzes. In the slides' example, tasks of length 10, 5, and 3 arriving at times 0, 6, and 8 give FIFO an average completion time of 43/3, about 14.33. Shortest-task-first with all three arriving at time 0 gives 29/3, about 9.67, and STF is provably optimal for average completion time. Round-robin is better for interactive work, while FIFO and STF suit batch work ([L23](https://courses.grainger.illinois.edu/cs425/fa2025/L23.FA25.pdf)). For Hadoop YARN, the **Capacity Scheduler** gives each queue a guaranteed share with soft and optional hard limits. It lets a queue grow elastically into idle capacity but *never preempts*, so it shrinks a queue only by waiting for tasks to finish. The **Fair Scheduler** gives each pool (usually one per user) an equal share and *does* preempt to restore minimum shares. It kills the most recently started tasks to waste the least work, which is safe only because map and reduce tasks are idempotent. Neither can use STF directly because task lengths aren't known in advance, so they estimate them from input size or from sibling tasks' runtimes.

**Dominant Resource Fairness** (DRF) handles multi-resource demands. A user's *dominant share* is the largest fraction of any single resource that the user holds, and DRF equalizes dominant shares across users. In the paper's example, a cluster has 9 CPUs and 18 GB. User A's tasks need 1 CPU and 4 GB, which is memory-dominant. User B's tasks need 3 CPUs and 1 GB, which is CPU-dominant. DRF gives A three tasks (3 CPUs, 12 GB) and B two tasks (6 CPUs, 2 GB), so each ends up with a dominant share of 2/3 ([Ghodsi et al., NSDI 2011](https://www.usenix.org/legacy/event/nsdi11/tech/full_papers/Ghodsi.pdf)). The lecture's version uses 18 CPUs and 36 GB and reaches the same 2/3 split ([L23](https://courses.grainger.illinois.edu/cs425/fa2025/L23.FA25.pdf)). The paper's four headline properties are sharing incentive, strategy-proofness (lying about your demand doesn't help you), envy-freeness, and Pareto efficiency. The authors implemented DRF in the Mesos cluster manager.

## For the podcast

The listener is an MCS student already in CS425 on Coursera, who has seen gossip, Chord, Paxos, and transactions. Spend most of the time on three mechanisms: Storm's anchoring and acking with the XOR ack val, Pregel supersteps with vote-to-halt and checkpoint rollback, and the DRF worked example. Treat the single-processor scheduling arithmetic and the Heron and ML material as brief asides. Say these names clearly: Pregel (PREH-gul), Nimbus, Supervisor, ZooKeeper, spout, bolt, Trident, Heron, Valiant's Bulk Synchronous Parallel, Ghodsi (GOHD-see), Zaharia (zah-HAR-ee-uh), and Mesos (MAY-sohs).

When explaining the XOR trick out loud, say "every tuple id gets folded in twice, once when it's born and once when it's acked, and anything XORed with itself vanishes, so the running value hits zero exactly when the whole tree is finished." For power laws, say "the number of nodes with k neighbors falls off like one over k to the alpha, which is a straight line on a log-log plot." For DRF, walk through "A's tasks are memory-hungry and B's are CPU-hungry, so give them tasks until A's share of memory equals B's share of CPU, and both land on two thirds."

Correct these misconceptions:
- Storm is at-least-once, not exactly-once, unless you use Trident.
- Shuffle grouping breaks stateful counting.
- Pregel messages are not delivered within the same superstep.
- Combiners are not on by default, and they're only safe for commutative and associative operations.
- Small-world and power-law are not the same thing.
- The Capacity Scheduler does not preempt, but the Fair Scheduler does.
- DRF does not give equal amounts of each resource. It equalizes each user's dominant share.
