They also draw on the primary sources those decks point to: the AWS and Facebook post-mortems and Needham and Schroeder's 1978 paper. The two halves fit together better than they first appear to. Each one asks what happens when your assumptions about the other party, or about timing, turn out to be wrong.

## Threats, properties, and the "golden A's"

L27 starts with three threats. **Leakage** is unauthorized access. **Tampering** is unauthorized modification. **Vandalism** is interference with no direct gain to the attacker, as in a denial-of-service attack. Each threat maps onto one CIA property: confidentiality, integrity, and availability, in that order. The concrete attacks are eavesdropping, masquerading, message tampering, replay, and DoS. Keep **policy** (what a secure system must achieve) separate from **mechanism** (how you achieve it: encryption, capabilities, and so on). The mechanisms are the three "golden A's." **Authentication** asks whether this really is Alice. **Authorization** asks whether Alice is allowed to do this. **Auditing** asks how Eve got in, and it depends on continuous logging. The deck also gives a four-step recipe for design. State an attacker model tied to reality, design mechanisms that meet the policy under that model, prove they do, and measure the cost in the no-attack common case (L27). Coulouris covers this material in Chapter 11. §11.2 is the overview, §§11.3–11.4 cover cryptographic algorithms and signatures, and §11.6 has case studies of Needham–Schroeder, Kerberos, and TLS ([CDK5 contents](https://www.cdk5.net/contents)).

## Symmetric vs. asymmetric, and what signatures really encrypt

In a symmetric system, one shared key K_AB both encrypts and decrypts. DES uses a 56-bit key on 64-bit blocks, and AES-256 is the current standard. In a public/private system, anything locked with K_A,priv opens only with K_A,pub, and the reverse also holds. RSA and PGP are the deck's examples. Expect exam questions on the trade-off. Shared keys are hard to revoke: removing one member of a group means everyone in the group has to change keys. Public-key operations are expensive, at least in one direction. So real systems use public keys to agree on a fresh shared key and then use that shared key for the bulk of the traffic (L27).

Signatures are where people mix up directions. To send a secret to Bob, encrypt with **Bob's public** key. To sign, encrypt with **your own private** key, which anyone can then check using your public key. In practice you sign a hash: [M, K_A,priv(Hash(M))]. That is fast, and it keeps the signature small. A certificate is just a signed statement binding a name to a value (an account number or a public key). Certificates chain from Alice's bank to the Bankers' Federation to a root such as Verisign, and verification has to end at a root you already trust (L27). For authorization, the deck reuses last week's DFS distinction. An **access control list** is kept per object at the server. A **capability list** is kept per principal, can be split into per-file capabilities, and can be handed to clients the way certificates are.

## Nonces, replays, and why the "optimized" protocol breaks

The protocol slides deserve real attention. In direct authentication with a shared key, each side sends a fresh random **nonce** and the other side proves it knows K_AB by returning that nonce encrypted. L27 then "optimizes" the exchange from five messages to three by piggybacking the nonces, and shows that the shorter version is open to a **reflection/replay attack**. Eve opens a session claiming to be Alice and receives Bob's challenge R_B. She then opens a *second* session and sends R_B back to Bob as her own challenge. Bob obligingly returns K_AB(R_B), which Eve uses to finish the first session. The lesson is not "fewer messages are bad." It is that a principal must never be made into an encryption oracle for its own challenges.

Needham and Schroeder's original paper is short and worth reading for the reasoning, not just the notation ([Needham & Schroeder 1978](https://research.cs.wisc.edu/areas/os/Qual/papers/authentication.pdf); [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/359657.359659)). Their attacker model is the one L27 asks you to state up front: the intruder "can interpose a computer in all communication paths," altering, copying, and replaying messages. In the authentication-server protocol, A sends (A, B, I_A1) in the clear. The server returns {I_A1, B, CK, {CK, A}K_B}K_A. A forwards the ticket {CK, A}K_B to B, and B finishes with a nonce handshake under CK. The paper explains why each field is there. Without B's name in the reply, an intruder could substitute X. Without the nonce, an old reply could be replayed to force reuse of an old conversation key. The paper also points out an asymmetry: B cannot tell whether CK is fresh "unless he remembers indefinitely keys previously used." That gap is exactly why the paper uses timestamps for mail, and it is the doorway to the Kerberos discussion in Coulouris §11.6. L27's indirect-authentication slide is the simplified version of this protocol. The AS hands out a "ticket" made of K_A,AS(K_AB) and K_B,AS(K_AB), and only Alice and Bob can open their halves.

## Outages: human error starts it, cascades keep it going

L28 opens with a survey answer: about **70%** of datacenter outages trace back to human error, not power or fire. The case studies all have the same shape. A small mistake triggers automated recovery logic, and that logic amplifies the damage.

**AWS EBS, April 2011.** During a routine capacity upgrade, traffic was shifted onto the low-capacity secondary network by mistake. EBS nodes lost contact with their replicas and "rapidly began searching the EBS cluster for available server space" to re-mirror. Free capacity ran out, and a **re-mirroring storm** hit about 13% of volumes in the AZ. Two things made it worse. The nodes "did not back off aggressively enough," and a low-probability **race condition** began firing under the extreme connection load ([AWS 2011](https://aws.amazon.com/message/65648/)). The control plane's long timeouts led to regional thread starvation, and in the end 0.07% of volumes were lost (L28). Customers who had architected across multiple AZs mostly escaped.

**Facebook, September 2010.** An automated checker replaced cache values it judged invalid with fresh values from the persistent store. Then someone pushed an invalid value *to the persistent store*. Every client tried to "fix" it, and the database cluster received hundreds of thousands of queries per second. Worse, clients treated a DB *error* as an invalid value and deleted the cache key, so the load fed itself: "a feedback loop that didn't allow the databases to recover" ([Johnson 2010](https://engineering.fb.com/2010/09/23/uncategorized/more-details-on-today-s-outage/)). The only fix was to take the site down and let users back in slowly. L28 draws the lesson as exponential backoff, waiting twice as long after each failure.

**The Planet, 2008.** A transformer short caused an explosion, and the fire department would not allow the backup generators to run. Taking 9K servers down is a physical failure that no replication inside the building can survive, so L28's lesson is cross-DC business continuity plans. The open question is who pays for them, the provider or the customer.

**AWS us-east-1, October 2025** is the case to know cold, because it is a CS425 concurrency bug. DynamoDB's DNS is managed by a Planner and three independent **Enactors**, one per AZ. An Enactor "makes a one-time check that its plan is newer than the previously applied plan" before it starts. One Enactor stalled. A second Enactor applied a much newer plan and then garbage-collected old plans. The slow Enactor then wrote its stale plan over the new one, because its freshness check was stale by the time it applied the plan. Cleanup then deleted that now-active old plan, which left the regional endpoint's DNS record empty ([AWS 2025](https://aws.amazon.com/message/101925/)). The failure then cascaded. EC2's DWFM lost its leases and hit "congestive collapse" while trying to re-establish them. Network Manager fell behind on the backlog. NLB health checks flip-flopped and removed capacity. L28 sums up the root cause as assuming your program will "run fast enough" so you need no concurrency control: "asynchrony is a way of life." It is check-then-act (a TOCTOU bug) on a replicated store, the same bug that optimistic concurrency control's validation phase exists to catch (Week 10).

The cross-cutting lessons in L28 are to test your failover (American Eagle found it couldn't fail over to its backup DC in a real disaster), to have a fallback plan for upgrades, to keep documentation consistent (a Google App Engine outage ran long because ops didn't know which doc version to use), and to recognize that outages are always a *chain* that needs breaking. L28 adds that as automation ("agents") takes on more operational work, the 70% human-error share may shift toward automated programs, which need their own guardrails.

## Programming tips for the C++ MPs

Several of these lessons show up directly in emulator code. Retry loops in your message handlers should **back off**: double the wait on each failure, up to a cap, rather than resending every tick. Never treat "no reply" or "error" as "the value is bad, so delete it," which is exactly the Facebook bug. When you apply an update that was decided earlier, re-check its version at the moment you write it, not only when you read it. Comparing incoming version or timestamp against the stored one at write time is the cheap fix for the Enactor bug. If your protocol uses request IDs, make them unique per transaction and reject duplicates, the same role nonces play. For DS4 the remaining programming deliverable is the Part 2 MP (due 12/7). ## For the podcast

The listener is an MCS student in CS425 who already knows replication, leases, Lamport and vector time, and optimistic concurrency control, so don't teach those again. Instead, connect them to the outages. Emphasize three things. First, the direction of keys: secrecy uses the receiver's public key, and signing uses the sender's private key. Second, the reflection attack on the three-message protocol. Third, the October 2025 DynamoDB race as a check-then-act bug. Mention the Planet fire and the human-error anecdotes only briefly, and skip the PGP key-block slide and the Paul Kwiat interview beyond one line (quantum-optics researcher, not exam material here). Say "Needham–Schroeder" (NEED-um SHRO-der), "nonce" (rhymes with "once," a number used once), "DWFM, the DropletWorkflow Manager," and "Enactor." Read formulas aloud as "K-A-B of R-B means R-B encrypted under the key Alice and Bob share" and "a signed message is M plus the hash of M encrypted with Alice's private key." Tell the Enactor story as two workers updating one notebook: the slow one checked the page date only once, then overwrote the fast one's newer page, and the fast one's cleanup tore the page out. Misconceptions to correct: encryption alone doesn't give authentication without freshness (nonces or timestamps); a signature is not "encrypting the message with the public key"; exponential backoff is a *client* fix, not a server feature; multi-AZ deployment helped in 2011 but didn't protect the regional control plane; and the 2025 outage was a concurrency bug in automation, not a DNS protocol failure.
