# CS410: Week 12 — Text-based prediction and contextual text mining (CPLSA, NetPLSA, causal topics)

This week closes the Text Mining half of the course by flipping the question. Until now the goal was to mine the *content* of text: words, topics, sentiment. Zhai's lecture 6.3 reframes all of it as one larger job: inferring the values of real-world variables, with text acting as data from human "sensors" alongside ordinary non-text sensors (Zhai, slides 6.3). The rest of the week (6.4–6.7) is the toolkit for that job, which Zhai calls *contextual text mining*: put the metadata around a document (time, place, author, network, a price series) directly into the topic model.

## The data mining loop and the two directions of joint analysis

The 6.3 slide that matters is the "data mining loop." Sensors, including people writing text, observe the real world. Joint mining of text and non-text data produces predictors (features). A predictive model turns those into predicted values of real-world variables. Those predictions drive decisions that change the world, and the loop starts again (Zhai, slides 6.3). Two framing points from that lecture are easy to test on a quiz. First, text-based prediction is presented as a *unified view* of everything earlier in the course: topic mining, opinion mining, and word association become sub-tasks that produce features, and mining "knowledge about the observer" (sentiment, perspective) is its own sub-task. Second, joint analysis runs in both directions. Non-text data helps text mining by supplying context, which is contextual text mining (Mei's 2009 thesis is the long-form reference). Text helps non-text mining by explaining patterns found elsewhere, which Zhai calls *pattern annotation* (Mei et al., KDD 2006, on semantic annotation of frequent patterns) (Zhai, slides 6.3).

## Context as a partition, and what CPLSA adds to PLSA

Lecture 6.4 defines context broadly. *Direct* context is metadata such as time, location, author, and source. *Indirect* context is anything reachable from it, such as the author's social network or age. "Any related data can be regarded as context." Context does two jobs: it *partitions* the collection for comparative analysis (papers from 1998, papers by U.S. authors, papers at SIGIR), and it *gives meaning* to discovered topics (Zhai, slides 6.4).

Contextual PLSA (Mei & Zhai, KDD 2006) is the general model. PLSA has one set of k topics and one topic mixture per document. CPLSA keeps k themes but allows two kinds of context dependence. A **view** is a context-specific version of every theme's word distribution, written \( \theta_{il} \) for theme \( l \) under view \( i \); comparing views gives *content variation* (how "retrieval models" is worded before vs. after 1998). A **coverage** is a distribution over themes, \( p(l \mid \kappa_j) \), tied to a context rather than to one document; comparing coverages gives *coverage variation* (how much Texas writes about "government response" vs. Louisiana). Each word is generated by choosing a view given the document's context, choosing a coverage given the context, choosing a theme from that coverage, and drawing the word from that view's version of the theme. The log-likelihood is

$$
\log p(\mathcal{D}) = \sum_{(D,C)} \sum_{w} c(w,D)\,\log \sum_{i=1}^{n} p(v_i \mid D,C) \sum_{j=1}^{m} p(\kappa_j \mid D,C) \sum_{l=1}^{k} p(l \mid \kappa_j)\, p(w \mid \theta_{il})
$$

and plain EM still fits it, with the hidden variable now indexing the (view, coverage, theme) triple (Mei & Zhai 2006). The lecture's summary line is the one to remember: context influences **both** the coverage and the content of topics (Zhai, slides 6.5). Expect it as a "which is NOT true" distractor.

Two practical gotchas come straight from the paper. CPLSA has many free parameters and many local maxima, so it only behaves when context hard-constrains it. For example, if views are disjoint time periods, \( p(\kappa_j \mid D,C) \) collapses to a delta function on the document's own period. The authors also pin the first theme to a single shared background unigram model to absorb stopwords, which they describe as an infinitely strong prior on that theme. They name two useful special cases. **Fixed-view CPLSA** shares one global word distribution per theme and varies only coverage. With time as the only context it yields *theme life cycles*; with time × location coverages it yields spatiotemporal theme analysis (Mei & Zhai 2006).

## Spatiotemporal trends and event impact

The Hurricane Katrina blog study (Mei, Liu, Su & Zhai, WWW 2006) is the source of the slide plots. A *theme life cycle* is the strength of a theme over time, roughly \( p(t \mid \theta) \). A *theme snapshot* is the distribution of themes over locations at a fixed time, drawn as a map. Rises in the "oil price" theme lining up with Hurricane Rita is the canonical example (Zhai, slides 6.5). *Event impact analysis* uses an event to split the timeline into before and after contexts and compares the views. In the paper's IR example, SIGIR abstracts are split at 1993 (first SIGIR after TREC began) and 1998 (the first language-modeling paper, Ponte & Croft). The "retrieval models" theme visibly shifts from vector/Boolean/relevance-feedback vocabulary toward language model, estimate, smooth, and likelihood (Mei & Zhai 2006; Zhai, slides 6.5). This is a nice bridge back to Week 4 of the course itself.

## NetPLSA: when the context is a graph

When context forms a network, such as coauthors, friends, or adjacent locations, Mei, Cai, Zhang & Zhai (WWW 2008) treat topic modeling as optimization and add a regularizer that says neighbors should have similar topic coverage:

$$
O(C,G) = -(1-\lambda)\sum_{d}\sum_{w} c(w,d)\log\sum_{j=1}^{k} p(\theta_j \mid d)\,p(w \mid \theta_j) \;+\; \frac{\lambda}{2}\sum_{(u,v)\in E} w(u,v)\sum_{j=1}^{k}\big(p(\theta_j \mid u)-p(\theta_j \mid v)\big)^2
$$

The objective is minimized. With \( \lambda = 0 \) it is plain PLSA. With \( \lambda = 1 \) it ignores text and clusters purely on graph structure. The regularizer is a graph-harmonic (Laplacian) smoothness term, and estimation is a *generalized* EM: the E-step and the \( p(w \mid \theta_j) \) update match PLSA, but the coverage update adds an inner loop that repeatedly mixes each node's topic distribution with its neighbors' weighted average (Mei et al. 2008). On DBLP authors from WWW, SIGIR, KDD, and NIPS, plain PLSA's four topics are muddled, while NetPLSA recovers clean IR, data-mining, machine-learning, and Web communities (Zhai, slides 6.6). The lecture's general recipe, "any generative model + any network + any regularizer," is the transferable idea (Zhai, slides 6.6).

## Causal topics: iterative topic modeling with time-series feedback (ITMTF)

Lecture 6.7 asks which topics in a text stream "matter" for an external time series, such as a stock price or the Iowa Electronic Markets presidential contract. The input is a time series plus timestamped text from the same period. The output is topics whose coverage correlates, possibly with a lag, with that series (Zhai, slides 6.7). Kim et al. (CIKM 2013) loop as follows. (1) Fit a topic model (PLSA in the paper) to get candidate topics. (2) Turn each topic's coverage into a daily series and test it against the external series with Pearson correlation or a Granger test, keeping topics above a significance cutoff and recording the best lag. (3) Inside each significant topic, test individual word-count streams. (4) Build a Dirichlet prior from the significant words weighted by significance, *splitting* a topic into a positive-impact and a negative-impact prior topic when it mixes both. (5) Refit with that prior at strength \( \mu \) and repeat. Their two quality measures are causality confidence and *purity*, an entropy-based score of how one-directional a topic's word impacts are. Higher \( \mu \) gave steadier purity gains over iterations (Kim et al. 2013).

Gotchas worth knowing. "Causal" means Granger-predictive, a statistical lead/lag relationship, not proven causation (Seth 2007 is the course's suggested reading). Granger tests need stationary inputs, and the paper smooths with a window-3 moving average and takes first differences before testing (Kim et al. 2013). Feeding a different series changes the topics you get: the same NYT corpus yields airline and terrorism topics for American Airlines stock but computer and Internet topics for Apple ("topics are biased toward each time series") (Zhai, slides 6.7).

## Programming notes

If you prototype any of this, start from your PLSA EM code. Fixed-view CPLSA with time as context only changes what the coverage is indexed by: replace per-document \( \pi_d \) with per-period \( \pi_t \) or keep both. NetPLSA's coverage update is a post-M-step smoothing pass, \( \pi_u \leftarrow (1-\gamma)\pi_u + \gamma \cdot \text{weighted average of neighbors' } \pi_v \), iterated until the objective stops improving (Mei et al. 2008). For ITMTF, `statsmodels.tsa.stattools.grangercausalitytests` is the Python counterpart of the R call the authors used, and the topic "stream" is just \( \sum_{d \in t} p(\theta_j \mid d) \) per day.

## For the podcast

**Audience.** MCS students already in CS410 who know PLSA and EM from Week 9. Don't re-teach PLSA. Spend the time on what each extension *adds*.

**Emphasize.** The data mining loop and "text as human sensors." The two directions of joint analysis (contextual text mining vs. pattern annotation). Views vs. coverage, and that context affects both. NetPLSA's lambda dial between pure text and pure graph. The ITMTF feedback loop. **Skip** the CPLSA EM update formulas, the Newton–Raphson details, and the experiment tables.

**Say clearly.** ChengXiang "Cheng" Zhai; Qiaozhu Mei; CPLSA as "contextual P-L-S-A"; NetPLSA as "net P-L-S-A"; ITMTF as "iterative topic modeling with time-series feedback"; Granger ("GRAIN-jer") causality; Iowa Electronic Markets; DBLP.

**Formulas out loud.** For CPLSA, say: pick a view based on the document's context, pick a coverage based on the context, pick a topic from that coverage, then pick a word from that view's version of the topic. For NetPLSA, say: one minus lambda times the PLSA log-likelihood, plus lambda times a penalty that adds up, over every edge, how different the two endpoints' topic mixes are, squared.

**Misconceptions.** First, context changes only topic *coverage*: false, since views change topic *content* too. Second, "causal topics" prove causation: false, since Granger tests show lead/lag predictiveness. Third, text-based prediction is just categorization: false, since the target is a real-world variable the text rarely states. Fourth, NetPLSA requires a social network: false, since any graph works, including a geographic adjacency graph.
