# CS410: Week 10 — Text clustering, k-means/HAC, and Naive Bayes categorization

Week 10 (Oct 26–Nov 1) is the course's turn from "find structure with no labels" to "use labels to sort text." Both halves reuse the machinery you already built: the unigram language model from Weeks 4 and 8 and the EM algorithm from Week 9. The cleanest way to hold the week in your head is that **a document-clustering mixture model and a Naive Bayes classifier are the same generative model**. The only difference is whether the cluster/category label of each training document is hidden (clustering, so you need EM) or observed (categorization, so you just count). This is also the syllabus's exam week, so the gotchas below are the ones most likely to show up as quiz-style questions.

## Clustering starts with a bias you have to choose

Zhai opens the clustering lectures with a point that sounds philosophical but drives everything after it: "similar" is not well defined until you pick a perspective. Are *car* and *horse* similar? As transportation, yes; as biology, no. He calls this the **clustering bias**, and it is the basis for evaluation, not a footnote (Zhai, Lec. 4.1, 4.6). In practice the bias lives in one of two places: inside a generative model (what kind of distribution a cluster is) or inside an explicit similarity function. The things you cluster can be whole documents, terms (to define "concepts"), passages, or even websites, and clusters can be clustered again to build a hierarchy (Lec. 4.1).

## The generative route: one topic per document

Take PLSA from last week and add one constraint: every document is generated by exactly one topic. To generate $d$, first pick cluster $\theta_i$ with probability $p(\theta_i)$, then generate *all* of $d$'s words from $p(w\mid\theta_i)$ (Lec. 4.2–4.3). That moves the sum outside the product:

$$
p(d\mid\Lambda)=\sum_{i=1}^{k} p(\theta_i)\prod_{w\in V} p(w\mid\theta_i)^{c(w,d)}
$$

Compare PLSA, where the product is outside, over words, each of which picks its own topic. This is the single most testable distinction in the week. A second one follows from it: in the clustering mixture $p(\theta_i)$ is a global cluster size and **does not depend on $d$**, unlike PLSA's per-document coverage $\pi_{d,j}$ (Lec. 4.3).

EM works almost the same as in Week 9, but the hidden variable is now one per *document*, $Z_d\in[1,k]$, not one per word. The E-step computes $p(Z_d=i\mid d)\propto p(\theta_i)\prod_w p(w\mid\theta_i)^{c(w,d)}$. The M-step sets $p(\theta_i)$ to the average of those posteriors over documents, and $p(w\mid\theta_i)$ to the counts $c(w,d_j)$ weighted by $p(Z_{d_j}=i\mid d_j)$ and then normalized (Lec. 4.4). Zhai's worked example is worth reproducing by hand. With equal priors, $p(\text{text}\mid\theta_1)=0.5$, $p(\text{mining}\mid\theta_1)=0.2$, both $0.1$ under $\theta_2$, and a document with two of each, the posterior for cluster 1 is $\frac{0.5\cdot0.5^2\cdot0.2^2}{0.5\cdot0.5^2\cdot0.2^2+0.5\cdot0.1^2\cdot0.1^2}=\frac{100}{101}$. For the M-step, posteriors of 0.9, 0.1, and 0.8 across three documents give $p(\theta_1)=0.6$ (Lec. 4.4). To get hard clusters at the end, assign $c_d=\arg\max_i p(d\mid\theta_i)$ (likelihood only), or $\arg\max_i p(d\mid\theta_i)p(\theta_i)$ if you want the prior to favor large clusters (Lec. 4.3).

**Programming gotcha: underflow.** A product of hundreds of word probabilities underflows to 0.0 in double precision, and the E-step ratio turns into 0/0. Zhai's fix is to divide every factor by a shared normalizer, such as the average $p(w\mid\bar\theta)$ across clusters. It cancels in the ratio but keeps the numbers representable (Lec. 4.4). In code, the usual equivalent is to work in log space and normalize with log-sum-exp:

```py
import numpy as np
from scipy.special import logsumexp

# X: (N_docs, |V|) count matrix; log_pw: (k, |V|); log_prior: (k,)
log_joint = log_prior[None, :] + X @ log_pw.T      # (N, k)
log_post  = log_joint - logsumexp(log_joint, axis=1, keepdims=True)
post = np.exp(log_post)                            # p(Z_d = i | d)
```

## The similarity route: HAC and k-means

If you'd rather state the bias directly, define a similarity function and look for a partition with high intra-cluster and low inter-cluster similarity (Lec. 4.5). **Hierarchical agglomerative clustering (HAC)** starts from singletons and repeatedly merges the most similar pair. Everything depends on how you score *group* similarity. **Single-link** uses the closest pair and gives loose clusters. **Complete-link** uses the farthest pair and gives tight clusters. Both are "individual decisions," so a single point can swing them. **Average-link** averages all pairs, a "group decision" that resists outliers (Lec. 4.5). Manning et al.'s slides make the failure modes concrete. Single-link *chains* into long, straggly clusters, and one outlier can produce a counterintuitive complete-link split. The naive HAC loop costs $O(N^3)$: $O(N)$ merges, each scanning $O(N^2)$ similarities (Schütze, IIR Ch. 17 slides).

**k-means** picks $k$ seed vectors, assigns each document to its nearest centroid, recomputes centroids, and repeats until the within-cluster sum of squares converges (Lec. 4.5). Zhai labels the assign step "E-step" and the recompute step "M-step" on purpose. k-means is hard-assignment EM. The real difference from the mixture model is that the mixture gives each document a *probability* for every cluster, while k-means commits it to one. Each step can only lower the residual sum of squares (RSS), so k-means always converges, but **only to a local minimum**, and bad seeds give bad clusterings (IIR Ch. 16 slides). Run several restarts. One more subtle trap: centroids are *not* length-normalized, so Euclidean distance and cosine can rank centroids differently even when your documents are unit vectors (IIR Ch. 16 slides).

## Evaluating clusters: directly or by what they buy you

**Direct evaluation** compares system clusters with a human gold-standard partition, using measures such as purity, normalized mutual information (NMI), or F (Lec. 4.6). **Purity** assigns each cluster its majority class and counts the hits. IIR's example scores $(5+4+3)/17\approx0.71$. But purity is 1.0 when every document is its own cluster, so it can't trade quality off against the number of clusters. Raw mutual information has the same flaw. NMI fixes it by dividing by $\tfrac{1}{2}[H(\Omega)+H(C)]$, and that same example scores only 0.36 (IIR §16.3). **Indirect evaluation** asks whether adding clustering to a baseline system improves that application's own metric. There the clustering bias comes from the application rather than from assessors (Lec. 4.6). Choosing $k$ is hard for every method, because the problem is unsupervised (Lec. 4.6).

## Categorization: the same model, with the labels showing

Text categorization assigns objects to predefined categories using labeled training data. The applications in the lectures are topic categorization, sentiment, spam filtering, email routing, MeSH indexing, and author attribution. Variants include binary, $k$-way, hierarchical, and joint categorization, and Zhai notes that binary categorization can in principle support all the others (Lec. 4.7). Hand-written rules work when categories have clean surface cues, but they don't scale and can't handle uncertainty, which is the motivation for learning "soft rules" from examples (Lec. 4.8).

**Naive Bayes** is the clustering mixture with $Z_d$ observed. Classify with

$$
\text{category}(d)=\arg\max_i\Big[\log p(\theta_i)+\sum_{w\in V}c(w,d)\log p(w\mid\theta_i)\Big]
$$

and train by counting: $p(\theta_i)=N_i/N$, and $p(w\mid\theta_i)$ is $w$'s share of all tokens in category $i$'s training documents (Lec. 4.9). The "naive" part is conditional independence of word positions given the class. It is badly violated, yet the classifier still works, because a correct decision only needs the right argmax, not calibrated probabilities (McCallum & Nigam 1998; IIR Ch. 13 slides).

**Smoothing is not optional.** One unseen word in a category gives $p(w\mid\theta_i)=0$, and the whole product goes to zero; IIR's slide puts it as "zero probabilities cannot be conditioned away." Zhai smooths both parameters: $p(\theta_i)=\frac{N_i+\delta}{N+k\delta}$ and $p(w\mid\theta_i)=\frac{c(w,T_i)+\mu\,p(w\mid\theta_B)}{\sum_{w'}c(w',T_i)+\mu}$. With a uniform background $1/|V|$ this becomes add-constant smoothing, and add-one is IIR's default. Zhai gives smoothing three jobs: fix sparsity, inject prior knowledge, and, via a background LM, supply **IDF-like discriminative weighting**, the same effect you saw with query-likelihood smoothing in Week 4 (Lec. 4.9; IIR Ch. 13 slides). For two classes the log-odds is a bias term plus $\sum_w c(w,d)\,\beta_w$ with $\beta_w=\log\frac{p(w\mid\theta_1)}{p(w\mid\theta_2)}$. Naive Bayes is a linear scorer over word counts (Lec. 4.9).

**Which Naive Bayes?** Two different event models both go by the name. The *multinomial* model is the unigram LM above, with counts. The *multivariate Bernoulli* model uses binary presence/absence and multiplies in the probability of every vocabulary word that is **absent** from the document. McCallum & Nigam found multinomial better at realistic vocabulary sizes, with about 27% lower error on average. Bernoulli was competitive only with small vocabularies (McCallum & Nigam 1998). When an MP or library says "Naive Bayes," check which one it means: `MultinomialNB` and `BernoulliNB` are not interchangeable.

## For the podcast

- **Audience:** UIUC MCS students already in CS410. They know unigram LMs, smoothing, PLSA, and EM from Weeks 4, 8, and 9, so don't re-teach EM from scratch. It's exam week, so a crisp, quiz-oriented tone helps.
- **Emphasize:** the "one topic per document" constraint as the bridge from PLSA to clustering and then to Naive Bayes; where the sum sits relative to the product; k-means as hard EM; why purity rewards singleton clusters; and smoothing as both a zero-fix and IDF-like weighting.
- **Skip or keep brief:** the full M-step formulas, HAC complexity details, and the rules-versus-learning motivation.
- **Say clearly:** "ChengXiang Zhai," "Hierarchical Agglomerative Clustering, or HAC," "k-means" (the letter k), "Naive Bayes," "Jelinek–Mercer" if smoothing comes up, "McCallum and Nigam," "Bernoulli," and "normalized mutual information, N-M-I."
- **Formulas out loud:** read the mixture likelihood as "sum over clusters of the cluster's prior times the product, over the document's words, of that word's probability under the cluster." Read the Naive Bayes rule as "pick the category that maximizes log prior plus, for each word, its count times the log of its probability in that category." Read the 100/101 example as "cluster one wins by a factor of a hundred."
- **Misconceptions to call out:** that clustering has one right answer (it depends on the chosen bias); that k-means finds the global optimum (it finds a local one); that high purity means a good clustering; that Naive Bayes needs independence to actually hold; that p(θ) in the clustering mixture is per document (it's global); and that "Naive Bayes" names just one model.
