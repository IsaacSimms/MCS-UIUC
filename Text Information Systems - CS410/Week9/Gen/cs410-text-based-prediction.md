# What does ChengXiang Zhai's lecture "Text-Based Prediction" actually teach?

## Answer

Video 6.3 is a 12-minute lecture titled "Text-Based Prediction." On the public record it is only a bridge: the CS 410 week-12 vocabulary names that task and a quoted "data mining loop," then spends the rest of the week on contextual text mining (Coursera 6.4–6.7). The slides, a transcript, and the body of textbook chapter 19 were not available, so the lecture's own definition, examples, loop diagram, and named obstacle are not recoverable from primary sources. The four draft claims all go past what those sources support. "Noisy sensor," "semantic gap," and the cycle collect → features → model → predict → act → adjust do not appear in the syllabus or in any 2016-or-earlier course source that could be opened. "Semantic gap" is Zhai's term in a 2018 paper and thesis. Topic models and sentiment analysis are inputs to an ordinary predictor in some forecasting work associated with this topic; they are not the method of the following videos, which put context inside the topic model.

## Findings

### Where the lecture sits

- Coursera "Text Mining and Analytics," Week 6, lists eight videos. 6.1 and 6.2 are Latent Aspect Rating Analysis (about 15 minutes each). 6.3 is "Text-Based Prediction" (12 minutes). 6.4 is "Contextual Text Mining: Motivation" (7 minutes). 6.5 is "Contextual Text Mining: Contextual Probabilistic Latent Semantic Analysis" (18 minutes). 6.6 is "Mining Topics with Social Network Context" (15 minutes). 6.7 is "Mining Casual Topics with Time Series Supervision" (20 minutes). 6.8 is "Course Summary" (19 minutes). — [T2] Coursera course page (https://www.coursera.org/learn/text-mining)
- The same Week 6 blurb says the module continues sentiment analysis and opinion mining with a focus on Latent Aspect Rating Analysis, then teaches joint mining of text and non-text data, including contextual text mining of topics together with time, location, authors, and sources, and ends with a course summary. — [T2] Coursera course page (https://www.coursera.org/learn/text-mining)
- The published CS 410 sample syllabus gives Week 12 as: text-based prediction, the "data mining loop," context (of text data), contextual text mining, contextual probabilistic latent semantic analysis (CPLSA), views of a topic, coverage of topics, spatiotemporal trends of topics, event impact analysis, network-regularized topic modeling, NetPLSA, causal topics, iterative topic modeling with time series supervision. Week 11 is the categorization, evaluation, and sentiment-classification vocabulary (opinion holder, opinion target, sentiment, n-grams, frequent patterns, overfitting). Week 10 already includes topic categorization and sentiment categorization. — [T2] CS 410 sample syllabus PDF, getfile.asp?id=515, modified 2020-12-22
- Latent Aspect Rating Analysis is videos 6.1–6.2. It is absent from the Week 12 keyword list. The KDD 2010 and 2011 LARA papers belong with that previous unit, not with video 6.3. — [T2] Coursera course page; [T2] CS 410 sample syllabus PDF

### What belongs to video 6.3, and only that

- From primary course materials, video 6.3 contributes two named ideas: the task name "text-based prediction," and the quoted phrase "the data mining loop." Both sit at the start of the Week 12 list, before "context (of text data)." — [T2] CS 410 sample syllabus PDF
- No instructor slide, transcript, or open chapter-19 passage was found that defines the task, lists the lecture's examples, draws the loop, or names an obstacle. Searches for Zhai plus "data mining loop" did not return an instructor definition. — [T2] CS 410 sample syllabus PDF, negative result of the slide and book-body search
- A later definition by the same instructor, December 2018, is not the lecture. It says text-based prediction takes free-form text and estimates a numerical response: a regression map from documents to response values. The examples there are restaurant ratings from comments, product sales from consumer feedback, and movie revenue from reviews. The thesis opening also names election forecasts, product sales, and box office. — [T1] Jiang, Zhai, and Mei, IEEE BigData 2018, Sections I–II (https://timan.cs.illinois.edu/czhai/pub/IEEE-BigData2018-KG.pdf); [T1] Jiang, UIUC PhD thesis, 2018, IDEALS opening (https://hdl.handle.net/2142/102463)
- That 2018 paper says the problem resembles text classification when both are set up as supervised learning, and that it is harder than topic categorization or sentiment analysis because the value to be predicted is not explicitly mentioned and the correlation with surface lexical features can be very weak. The running example is product reviews used to predict sales, when the sales figures are very unlikely to be in the reviews. — [T1] Jiang, Zhai, and Mei, IEEE BigData 2018, Section I
- The same 2018 paper is where "semantic gap" is defined: a gap between the target variable and the surface features usually used to represent text. The obstacle it names is how to bridge that gap. The proposed bridge is a knowledge graph, which is later research, not a Week 12 technique. The phrase "semantic gap" does not occur in the Week 12 keyword list. It was not found in any opened 2016-or-earlier course source. Treat it as a 2018 gloss unless chapter 19 or the slides later show otherwise. — [T1] Jiang, Zhai, and Mei, IEEE BigData 2018, abstract and Section I; [T2] CS 410 sample syllabus PDF
- The 2018 wording for the sensor metaphor is "data reported by human sensors," with the claim that humans are far more intelligent than physical sensors. It does not call text a noisy sensor. — [T1] Jiang, Zhai, and Mei, IEEE BigData 2018, abstract
- Noah A. Smith's whitepaper of 1 March 2010, often copied onto student reproductions of a CS 410 reading list, was not printed on the public syllabus, so its assignment to this week is unverified. On its own terms it defines a different name, text-driven forecasting: given text pertinent to a social phenomenon, predict a measurement of that phenomenon that can be obtained only in the future. Its worked examples are pre-release movie reviews predicting opening-weekend per-screen gross; Form 10-K text predicting later return volatility; political blog posts predicting who comments and how much comment text appears; and a Twitter positive-to-negative word ratio tracked against Gallup's economic confidence index, with a mention of presidential approval. It also points at search queries for flu trends and floor-debate transcripts for votes. It says text is noisy, unstructured, and sparse. It does not use "sensor" or "semantic gap." — [T1] Smith, Text-Driven Forecasting, 1 March 2010, Sections 1–6 (http://www.cs.cmu.edu/~nasmith/papers/smith.whitepaper10.pdf)
- Smith separates sentiment classification of a review (is the critic positive?) from predicting what the film will earn. His forecasters are ordinary supervised models: elastic-net linear regression on words, bigrams, trigrams, and dependency relations; support-vector regression on words and bigrams; and, for blogs, latent Dirichlet allocation topics inside a predictor, compared with a bag-of-words naive Bayes baseline. That is the one primary source in this set that treats earlier text analyses as features for an ordinary predictor. It is not a transcript of video 6.3. — [T1] Smith, Text-Driven Forecasting, Sections 2–5
- The book's own catalog description, which is about the whole book rather than this lecture, says humans understand natural language far better than computers, so a text information system generally needs humans in it and often serves as an assistant. That is the nearest primary statement of why a person stays involved. It does not define a "data mining loop," and it does not give the cycle collect → features → model → predict → act → adjust. That cycle was not found in any opened source. — [T2] Morgan & Claypool catalog for DOI 10.1145/2915031
- The public Week 6 blurb introduces joint analysis as joint mining of text and non-text, and then names contextual text mining (non-text context used while analyzing text). It does not use the phrase "pattern annotation." — [T2] Coursera course page
- "Pattern annotation" is the keyword of a different Zhai-group paper: generating semantic annotations for frequent patterns by building a context model of a pattern, picking context indicators, and extracting representative transactions and similar patterns. That paper is about interpreting frequent itemsets. It is not in the Week 12 keyword list, and nothing opened equates it with "text explains patterns found in non-text data." — [T1] Mei, Xin, Cheng, Han, and Zhai, KDD 2006, abstract (https://timan.cs.illinois.edu/czhai/pub/kdd06-sem.pdf)

### Videos 6.4–6.7, at course-note depth

These notes follow the papers whose problem names are the syllabus terms and the Coursera titles. The videos themselves were not transcribed, so this is the technique the week names, not a slide-by-slide script.

- Contextual text mining, in the thesis the video's line of work comes from, treats context as a first-class part of mining. Explicit context includes time, location, and authors. Implicit context includes the sentiment an author had while writing. Complex context includes a social network. The point is to discover how topics vary across those contexts. Sample uses in the thesis abstract: search logs in the context of a user, reviews in the context of sentiment, and blogs or papers in the context of a social network. — [T1] Mei, Contextual Text Mining, PhD thesis, 2009, abstract (https://ideals.illinois.edu/bitstream/2142/14707/1/Mei_Qiaozhu.pdf)
- The CPLSA paper's working notion of context is any metadata value. Documents that share a value are in the same context. A document can sit in several contexts at once, and any combination of metadata is a complex context. Examples named there: time, location, author, source of a news article, age group, occupation, and citation frequency. The mining task is to recover themes and then compare them across contexts. — [T1] Mei and Zhai, KDD 2006, Sections 1–2 (https://timan.cs.illinois.edu/czhai/pub/kdd06-mix.pdf)
- The author's own course-note gloss of that paper: contextual text mining means (1) extract topics and (2) model two kinds of variation over context, content variation (a view) and strength variation (coverage). Any context that partitions the collection can be used. Time, location, authorship, and events are the examples he lists, and several earlier models are special cases. — [T3] Mei, publication notes, KDD 2006 entry (https://websites.umich.edu/~qmei/pub_by_topic.html)
- CPLSA extends PLSA by letting context choose which view and which coverage generate the words. A view is a set of word distributions for the themes, so the same theme can be worded differently in different contexts. A coverage is a distribution over themes, so a theme can be strong in one context and weak in another. A global view, shared by the whole collection, is kept so there is still one common version of each theme. Parameters are still estimated with expectation-maximization. Two useful restrictions: fixed coverage, when the question is how the wording changes (theme evolution; common themes versus collection-specific themes); fixed view, when the question is how the strength changes (theme life cycles; a theme's strength across time and place). — [T1] Mei and Zhai, KDD 2006, Sections 2–3
- Cross-collection comparison is the fixed-coverage special case with one global view and one view per subcollection, so shared themes can be set beside themes specific to each collection. The paper points to Zhai, Velivelli, and Yu, KDD 2004, for that comparison. The 2004 paper itself was not opened. — [T1] Mei and Zhai, KDD 2006, Section 3.2
- Spatiotemporal trends, in the same paper's fixed-view experiment, use blog posts retrieved for Hurricane Katrina, with week and U.S. state as context. Themes stay globally worded; what changes is coverage. One illustrated theme is aid and donations, mapped across states over six weeks. A separate view comparison on the same disaster says that in directly affected states the aid theme shifts toward medical care, and in the later weeks toward rebuilding and returning evacuees. The earlier WWW 2006 weblog paper is the detailed Katrina and Rita study this experiment compresses: theme life cycles (strength over time at a place) and theme snapshots (strength over places at a time), including government response, New Orleans, oil price, and the observation that Katrina discussion in Louisiana rose again as Rita approached. — [T1] Mei and Zhai, KDD 2006, Section 4.2; [T1] Mei, Liu, Su, and Zhai, WWW 2006, Sections 1 and 4 (https://timan.cs.illinois.edu/czhai/pub/www06-blog.pdf)
- Event impact analysis, as that paper defines the use of CPLSA, compares themes in documents from before an event with themes from after it. The demonstration is 28 years of SIGIR abstracts and two events: the start of TREC in 1992, and the introduction of language models into information retrieval in 1998. Contexts are allowed to overlap (for example, everything before 1998 overlaps everything before 1993). A retrieval-model theme moves from vector-space and Boolean wording before TREC, toward collection, judgment, and ranking wording after TREC, and toward language-model, likelihood, and smoothing wording after 1998. — [T1] Mei and Zhai, KDD 2006, Section 4.3
- Another CPLSA view comparison in the same paper uses author plus time on abstracts by two data-mining researchers, and shows one frequent-pattern theme worded differently for each author and each era. That is author as a context label. It is not yet a network. — [T1] Mei and Zhai, KDD 2006, Section 4.1
- Network-regularized topic modeling (video 6.6, syllabus term NetPLSA) applies when the context is a graph: co-authorship, friendship, citation, email reply, links, or a geographic network. The paper's rule is that vertices connected by an edge should carry similar topic weights. The objective mixes ordinary topic-model likelihood with a graph smoother. Turning the smoother off leaves ordinary PLSA. With PLSA as the topic model, the method is called NetPLSA. The result is a set of topics, a map of each topic onto the network, and topical communities. The paper's claim for doing both together is that text-only models ignore the edges, and network-only community methods have no guarantee of a coherent topic. — [T1] Mei, Cai, Zhang, and Zhai, WWW 2008, abstract and Sections 1 and 3 (https://timan.cs.illinois.edu/czhai/pub/www08-net.pdf)
- The same author's gloss of that benefit: the network smoother makes topics agree locally with the graph, and the topic model ties distant parts of the graph together through the text. He names topical communities and topic maps as the output. — [T3] Mei, publication notes, WWW 2008 entry (https://websites.umich.edu/~qmei/pub_by_topic.html)
- Causal topics (video 6.7) are defined computationally, not philosophically: a topic that is semantically coherent and strongly associated with an external time series, possibly with a lag. A positive or negative lag means the topic might lead the series or follow it. The paper says it is identifying candidate associations for an analyst; what "cause" means depends on the application. Motivating pairs are news with stock prices, ads or reviews with sales, and campaign news with polls. — [T1] Kim et al., CIKM 2013, abstract and Sections 1 and 3
- The method the syllabus calls iterative topic modeling with time series supervision works as follows, in the paper's own outline. Build topics. Test which topics correlate with the series, including lags. Inside those topics, test which frequent words correlate, and keep the sign of the impact. Build a prior that favors those words, and split words with positive impact from words with negative impact so they are not forced into one topic. Run the topic model again under that prior. Repeat. A topic's time series is its total coverage in the documents of each time bin. The paper's point is that the time series has to steer topic formation; discovering topics first and correlating second uses the same topics no matter which series you care about. Experiments use news against prices from the Iowa Electronic Markets and against American Airlines and Apple stock prices. This is supervision of the topic model. It is not "extract topics, then hand them to a separate predictor." — [T1] Kim et al., CIKM 2013, Sections 1 and 4

## Recommendation

Write the notes in two blocks, and keep the draft's four slogans out of both.

Block A, video 6.3 only: the course names the task text-based prediction and a "data mining loop," and it places both after categorization and sentiment and before contextual mining. State that the slide definition, the examples, the loop's steps, and the reason a person stays in the loop were not in the public sources. If a later sentence is added from the 2018 paper or from Smith, label it as later research or as an outside whitepaper, not as the lecture.

Block B, videos 6.4–6.7: use the syllabus order. Context partitions text. CPLSA adds context-dependent views (wording) and coverage (strength) on top of PLSA, still fit by expectation-maximization. Spatiotemporal trends are the Katrina coverage maps. Event impact is before-versus-after wording, with TREC and the 1998 language-model paper as the paper's example. NetPLSA adds a network smoother so linked authors or places share topic weight. Causal topics are topics steered by a time series through an iterative prior, including lag and sign. Leave LARA in the previous unit.

The tradeoff is completeness against invention. Filling 6.3 from the 2018 "semantic gap" paper or from the draft loop would make the notes sound like the lecture and teach a later research agenda, plus a pipeline story that the causal-topic video is written to go beyond.
